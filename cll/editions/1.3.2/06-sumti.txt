## 6.1 The five kinds of simple sumti

If you understand anything about Lojban, you know what a sumti is by now, right? An argument, one of those things that fills the places of simple Lojban sentences like:

[Example 6.1]

mi klama lo zarci

I go-to the market

In [example-random-id-VKU6] , mi and lo zarci are the sumti. It is easy to see that these two sumti are not of the same kind: mi is a pro-sumti (the Lojban analogue of a pronoun) referring to the speaker, whereas lo zarci is a description which refers to a market.

There are five kinds of simple sumti provided by Lojban:

1. descriptions like lo zarci , which usually begin with a descriptor (called a gadri in Lojban) such as lo ;

2. pro-sumti, such as mi ;

3. names, such as la .lojban. , which usually begin with la ;

4. quotations, which begin with lu , lo'u , zo , or zoi ;

5. pure numbers, which usually begin with li.

Here are a few examples of each kind of sumti:

[Example 6.2]

e'osai ko sarji la .lojban.

[request]--[!] You--[imperative] support that-named Lojban.

Please support Lojban!

[example-random-id-Dx1s] exhibits ko , a pro-sumti; and la .lojban. , a name.

[Example 6.3]

mi cusku lu e'osai li'u lo tcidu

I express [quote] [request]--[!] [unquote] to-the reader.

I express "Please!" to the reader.

[example-random-id-v1mS] exhibits mi , a pro-sumti; lu e'osai li'u , a quotation; and lo tcidu , a description.

[Example 6.4]

ti mitre li ci

This measures-in-meters the-number three.

This is three meters long.

[example-random-id-0YaH] exhibits ti , a pro-sumti; and li ci , a number.

Most of this chapter is about descriptions, as they have the most complicated syntax and usage. Some attention is also given to names, which are closely interwoven with descriptions. Pro-sumti, numbers, and quotations are described in more detail in [chapter-anaphoric-cmavo] , [chapter-mekso] , and [chapter-structure] respectively, so this chapter only gives summaries of their forms and uses. See [section-pro-sumti] through [section-number-summary] for these summaries.


## 6.2 The three basic description types

The following cmavo are discussed in this section:

lo | LE | the generic article: one or more things which really are

le | LE | the specific article: the one(s) described as

la | LA | the name article: the one(s) named

ku | KU | elidable terminator for LE, LA

The syntax of descriptions is fairly complex, and not all of it can be explained within the confines of this chapter: relative clauses, in particular, are discussed in [chapter-relative-clauses]. However, most descriptions have just two components: a descriptor belonging to selma'o LE or LA, and a selbri. (The difference between selma'o LE and selma'o LA is not important until [section-names].) Furthermore, the selbri is often just a single brivla. Here is an elementary example:

[Example 6.5]

lo zarci

one-or-more-things-which-really are-markets

a market

the market

markets

The word lo is Lojban's generic article, and the workhorse of everyday Lojban: when in doubt about which descriptor to use, use lo. A description built with it refers to something – one thing or several, the word itself does not say – which genuinely fits the description. The several English translations shown above are all correct: unlike English, Lojban does not force a choice between "a market", "the market", and "markets". Only context tells us which is meant. (This does not mean Lojban has no way of specifying the number of markets: that mechanism is explained in [section-quantified-descriptions].)

The general purpose of all descriptors is to create a sumti which refers to something that might occur in the x1 place of the selbri belonging to the description. Thus lo zarci refers to something which could be found in the x1 place of zarci – a market, or several.

The central property of lo is that it is "veridical": whatever lo zarci refers to must really be a market – that is, it must be something which can appear in the x1 place of a truthful bridi whose selbri is zarci. Thus

[Example 6.6]

lo mlatu cu gerku

One-or-more-things-which-really-are cats - is/are-dogs.

The cat is a dog.

Some cats are dogs.

must be false in Lojban: whatever things lo mlatu picks out are really cats, and no cat is a dog. Pointing at some specific cats or dogs would not make [example-random-id-fSxN] true, because those specific individuals are no more both-cat-and-dog than any others. In general, lo refers to whatever actually fits its description.

It is worth pausing on what a description like lo zarci does and does not say. It refers: it picks out some market or markets, and the rest of the sentence then talks about them. It does not say how many they are, or which ones they are, or whether they do things together or separately. All of those questions have answers only if the speaker chooses to supply them – by adding a number ([section-quantified-descriptions]), or by using one of the more specialized descriptors explained later in this chapter. This reticence is not vagueness for its own sake: most of the time, context answers such questions effortlessly, just as it does for the missing number distinction in English "the sheep".

The second descriptor of this section is le. Unlike lo , le is specific:

[Example 6.7]

le zarci

one-or-more-specific-things-each-of-which-I-describe-as being-a-market

the market

The long gloss for le is of course far too long to use most of the time, and in fact le is quite close in meaning to English "the". It has particular implications, however, which "the" does not have.

The specific purpose of le is twofold. First, it indicates that the speaker has one or more specific markets in mind (whether or not the listener knows which ones they are). Second, it also indicates that the speaker is merely describing the things he or she has in mind as markets, without being committed to the truth of that description.

[Example 6.8]

le mlatu cu gerku

One-or-more-specific-things-which-I-describe-as "cats" - is/are-dogs.

The cat is a dog.

The cats are dogs.

Unlike [example-random-id-fSxN] , [example-random-id-PutX] is not self-contradictory in Lojban, because le mlatu merely means something or other which, for my present purposes, I choose to describe as a cat, whether or not it really is a cat. A plausible instance would be: some animal we had assumed to be a cat at a distance turned out to be actually a dog on closer observation. [example-random-id-PutX] is what I would say to point out my observation to you. This property of le is called "non-veridicality": a le description reflects the speaker's way of identifying things, not a claim about what they really are.

In all descriptions with le , the listener is presumed to either know what I have in mind or else not to be concerned at present (perhaps I will give more identifying details later). In particular, I might be pointing at the supposed cat or cats: [example-random-id-PutX] would then be perfectly intelligible, since le mlatu merely clarifies that I am pointing at the supposed cat, not at a landscape, or a nose, which happens to lie in the same direction.

So the choice between lo and le turns on two independent questions: is the description offered as really true of the things referred to (veridical lo versus descriptive le)? And is the speaker singling out particular things he or she has in mind (specific le versus generic lo)? When neither specificity nor a possibly-loose description is wanted – which is most of the time – lo is the right choice.

The last descriptor of this section is la , which indicates that the selbri which follows it has been dissociated from its normal meaning and is being used as a name. Like le descriptions, la descriptions are implicitly restricted to those I have in mind. (Do not confuse this use of la with its use before regular Lojbanized names, which is discussed in [section-names].) For example:

[Example 6.9]

la cribe pu finti le lisri

That-named "bear" [past] creates the story.

Bear wrote the story.

In [example-random-id-PrGp] , la cribe refers to someone whose naming predicate is cribe , i.e. "Bear". In English, most names don't mean anything, or at least not anything obvious. The name "Frank" coincides with the English word "frank" , meaning "honest" , and so one way of translating "Frank ate some cheese" into Lojban would be:

[Example 6.10]

la stace pu citka lo cirla

That-named "Honest/Frank" [past] eats some cheese.

English-speakers typically would not do this, as we tend to be more attached to the sound of our names than their meaning, even if the meaning (etymological or current) is known. Speakers of other languages may feel differently. (In point of fact, "Frank" originally meant "the free one" rather than "the honest one".)

It is important to note the differences between [example-random-id-PrGp] and the following:

[Example 6.11]

le cribe pu finti le lisri

One-or-more-specific-things-which-I-describe-as bears [past] creates the story.

The bear(s) wrote the story.

[Example 6.12]

lo cribe pu finti le lisri

One-or-more-real-bears [past] creates the story.

A bear wrote the story.

Bears wrote the story.

[example-random-id-nXyo] is about a specific bear or bearlike thing(s), or thing(s) which the speaker (perhaps whimsically or metaphorically) describes as a bear (or more than one); [example-random-id-93Yv] is about one or more genuine bears, and says nothing about which bears they are. If several bears are meant, neither example settles whether each of them wrote separately or they collaborated on the story; Lojban has ways of making that explicit (see [section-masses]), but plain descriptions leave it to context.

(The notion of a "real bear" raises certain difficulties. Is a panda bear a real bear? How about a teddy bear? In general, the answer is "yes". Lojban gismu are defined as broadly as possible, allowing tanru and lujvo to narrow down the definition. There probably are no necessary and sufficient conditions for defining what is and what is not a bear that can be pinned down with complete precision: the real world is fuzzy. What counts is what the speaker and listener are prepared to treat as a bear in the current conversation. In borderline cases, le may communicate better than lo.)

So while [example-random-id-PrGp] could easily be true (there is a real writer named "Greg Bear"), and [example-random-id-nXyo] could be true if the speaker is sufficiently peculiar in what he or she describes as a bear, [example-random-id-93Yv] is false unless some genuine bear has taken up authorship.

Similarly, compare the following two examples, which are analogous to [example-random-id-nXyo] and [example-random-id-93Yv] respectively:

[Example 6.13]

le remna pu finti le lisri

Those-described-as humans [past] write the story.

The human being(s) wrote the story.

[Example 6.14]

lo remna pu finti le lisri

One-or-more-real-humans [past] write the story.

A human being wrote the story.

Human beings wrote the story.

[example-random-id-qKw7] says who the author of the story is: one or more particular human beings that the speaker has in mind. If the topic of conversation is the story, then [example-random-id-qKw7] identifies the author as someone who can be pointed out or who has been previously mentioned; whereas if the topic is a person, then le remna is in effect a shorthand reference to that person. [example-random-id-qKYf] merely says that the author is human – a natural thing to say when nothing more is known about the author, or when nothing more is relevant.

The meaning of lo given here is the fruit of a reform the community calls "xorlo", after the nickname of its principal author; it is now the ratified standard. If you learned your Lojban from the first edition of this book, the articles are the biggest thing that has changed – the old definitions, and how to read older text, are laid out in [appendix-changes].

The elidable terminator for all descriptions is ku. It can almost always be omitted with no danger of ambiguity. The main exceptions are in certain uses of relative clauses, which are discussed in [section-descriptors] , and in the case of a description immediately preceding the selbri. In this latter case, using an explicit cu before the selbri makes the ku unnecessary. There are also a few other uses of ku: in the compound negator naku (discussed in [chapter-quantifiers]) and to terminate place-structure, tense, and modal tags that do not have associated sumti (discussed in [chapter-sumti-tcita] and [chapter-tenses]).


## 6.3 Individuals and groups

The following cmavo are discussed in this section:

loi | LE | a group of those which really are

lei | LE | the group I describe as

lai | LA | the group of those named

Suppose we say:

[Example 6.15]

lo ci prenu cu bevri le pipno

The three persons carry the piano.

Three people carry the piano.

The number ci placed inside the description simply tells us that we are talking about three people; this mechanism is explained fully in [section-quantified-descriptions]. I will assume that there is only one piano under discussion.

What exactly is being claimed? There are two rather different situations that [example-random-id-mwhq] might describe. Perhaps each of the three carried the piano, alone, one after another – three separate feats of strength. Philosophers call this the "distributive" reading: the carrying is distributed over the individuals, each satisfying the selbri by himself or herself. Or perhaps – pianos being heavy – person 1 lifted one end, person 2 the other, and person 3 held up the middle, so that no one of them can be said to have carried the piano alone: only all three together did. This is the "collective" reading. [example-random-id-mwhq] is neutral between the two: like number, this is something a plain description simply does not specify, and context usually settles it. (For a piano, the collective reading is the likely guess; if the x2 were a suitcase, we would probably understand the distributive one.)

Lojban can, however, make collectivity explicit. That is the job of the descriptors loi , lei , and lai , which refer to "groups" – in Lojban, gunma. (Older texts say "masses", and mean something rather different by it; see [appendix-changes].)

[Example 6.16]

lei ci prenu cu bevri le pipno

The-group-of three persons carries the piano.

The three people carry the piano together.

Here the three persons are treated as a single unit, a group, and it is the group that does the carrying. A group satisfies a selbri collectively: the claim is true of the group as a whole, and there is no implication that any member satisfies it alone. Indeed, in our piano-moving scene none of the three individually carries the piano – person 3 may not even be lifting anything, merely steadying it – yet [example-random-id-x6Ba] is straightforwardly true.

A group is a thing in its own right, with its own properties. Some of its properties arise from joint action of its members, like carrying the piano; others belong to the group as such – the group of three people weighs 200 kilograms, has three members, and perhaps has existed as a piano-moving team for years, none of which is true of any single member. And many properties of the members do not carry over to the group at all: each of the three may be strong, while the group as a team is clumsy. A group simply has whatever properties it has, and logic applies to statements about groups exactly as to statements about anything else.

The descriptors loi and lai are related to lo and la just as lei is related to le : lei ci prenu is a group of specific individuals whom the speaker describes as persons, while loi ci prenu is a group of three genuine persons, without any indication of which ones:

[Example 6.17]

loi so'i tadni cu sruri lo dinju

A-group-of many students surrounds the building.

Many students are surrounding the building.

Some selbri practically demand a collective subject: sruri is a good example, since no single student can surround a building. With such predicates the group descriptors are the natural choice. (The number so'i means "many"; placed inside the description, it gives the size of the group.)

One thing you do not need a group descriptor for is general statements about kinds. Plain lo does that job by itself:

[Example 6.18]

lo cinfo cu xabju lo fi'ortu'a

Lions dwell-in Africa.

The lion dwells in Africa.

Lions dwell in Africa.

This is a natural consequence of lo being fully generic: a statement about lo cinfo can be a statement about lions in general, or lions as a kind, and it is not falsified by the existence of zoo lions elsewhere, any more than the English "Lions dwell in Africa" is. Similarly, English words for substances translate smoothly:

[Example 6.19]

lo matne cu ranti

Butter is-soft.

Butter is soft.

Here lo matne refers to butter as such – not to any particular pat of it, and not to a count of butter-objects. That some particular butter is frozen hard does not make [example-random-id-x6Bc] false, any more than a soft banana falsifies "bananas are yellow". How far such generic reference stretches – kinds, substances, abstractions – is deliberately left open by the language definition; speakers differ, and context does most of the work.

The group name descriptor lai is used in circumstances where we wish to talk about a group of things identified by a name which is common to all of them. It is not used to identify a group by a single name peculiar to it. Thus:

[Example 6.20]

lai cribe pu finti lo vi cukta

The-group-of-those-named "bear" [past] creates the nearby book.

The Bears wrote this book.

in a context where la cribe would be understood as referring to several people – say, Tom Bear and Fred Bear – means that the Bears wrote the book as collaborators: the group did the writing. Using la instead of lai in [example-random-id-H8z5] would leave the question open, exactly as with lo ci prenu and the piano.

Groups may also be built out of specific named or listed individuals with the connective joi (see [chapter-connectives]) and converted to and from other kinds of reference with the qualifiers of [section-sumti-qualifiers]. The underlying selbri for groups is gunma : x1 is a group with members x2; a sumti like loi ci prenu means neither more nor less than lo gunma be lo ci prenu , a group whose members are three people.


## 6.4 Groups and sets

The following cmavo are discussed in this section:

lo'i | LE | a set of those which really are

le'i | LE | the set described as

la'i | LA | the set of those named

Having said so much about groups, let us turn to sets. A set, in the mathematical sense, is an abstract object determined entirely by its membership: it has properties like cardinality (how many elements it has), membership (the relationship between a set and its elements), and inclusion (the relationship between a set and its subsets), and essentially no others. This makes sets quite different from groups. A group of three people can carry a piano and weigh 200 kilograms; the set of those three people carries nothing and weighs nothing, for a set is not the sort of thing that acts or weighs. The set descriptors le'i , lo'i and la'i correspond to the group descriptors lei , loi , and lai respectively. Here are some examples contrasting lo , loi , and lo'i:

[Example 6.21]

lo ratcu cu bunre

Rats are-brown.

Rats are brown.

[Example 6.22]

loi ratcu cu terlu'i lo panka

A-group-of rats cleans the park.

Rats (as a team) clean the park.

[Example 6.23]

lo'i ratcu cu barda

The-set-of rats is-large.

There are a lot of rats.

[example-random-id-x6Ca] says something about rats in general, each or any of them – brownness is a property of individual rats. [example-random-id-x6Cb] (an unlikely municipal arrangement, but a grammatical one) attributes the cleaning to a group of rats collectively: no single rat cleans the park. [example-random-id-x6Cc] is about the set of rats, and largeness here is cardinality: the set has billions of members. It would be incorrect to call the set of rats brown – brown-ness is not the sort of property that sets possess. Note that a bare lo'i ratcu refers to a set of rats supplied by context; taken in the broadest context it is naturally read as the set of all rats there are, but that reading comes from context, not from a built-in "all".

Lojban speakers should generally think twice before employing the set descriptors: if the idea is that some things do something together, the group descriptors are wanted instead. However, certain predicates have places that require set sumti to fill them. For example, the place structure of fadni is:

1

2

3

Why is it necessary for the x3 place of fadni to be a set? Because it makes no sense for an individual to be typical of another individual: an individual is typical relative to a whole population. In order to make sure that the bridi containing fadni is about an entire population, its x3 place must be filled with a set:

[Example 6.24]

mi fadni zo'e lo'i lobypli

I am-ordinary in-property-[unspecified] among-the-set-of Lojban-users.

I am a typical Lojban user.

Note that the x2 place has been omitted; I am not specifying in exactly which way I am typical – whether in language knowledge, or age, or interests, or something else. If lo'i were changed to lo in [example-random-id-xIXo] , the whole point would be lost: lo lobypli supplies a person or persons, and one cannot be typical of a person – the x3 of fadni wants the population as a set, not its members.

The underlying selbri for sets is selcmi : a sumti like lo'i ratcu means the same as lo selcmi be lo ratcu , a set whose members are rats. Since a set is determined by its members, a set of things can only be formed when it is determinate what its members are; sets of vaguely generic or substance-like referents do not arise in practice.


## 6.5 Descriptors for typical objects

A caution before anything else in this section: lo'e and le'e are the least settled words in this chapter. The language's definition process never finalized their semantics; what follows is the traditional account, essentially that of the first edition, and it is not universally accepted – some speakers prefer to express typicality with ordinary generic lo (see [section-masses]) or with the selbri fadni. They are also, in practice, rarely used. Read this section as a description of a convention, not a settled rule.

The following cmavo are discussed in this section:

lo'e | LE | the typical

le'e | LE | the stereotypical

On the traditional account, then, Lojban has a method for discriminating between "the lion" who lives in Africa and "the Englishman" who, generally speaking, doesn't live in Africa even though some Englishmen do. The descriptor lo'e is glossed "the typical" , as in

[Example 6.25]

lo'e cinfo cu xabju lo fi'ortu'a

The-typical lion - dwells-in the African-land.

The lion dwells in Africa.

What is this "typical lion" ? Surely it is not any particular lion, because no lion has all of the "typical" characteristics, and (worse yet) some characteristics that all real lions have can't be viewed as typical. For example, all real lions are either male or female, but it would be bizarre to suppose that the typical lion is either one. So the typical lion has no particular sex, but does have a color (golden brown), a residence (Africa), a diet (game), and so on. Likewise we can say that

[Example 6.26]

lo'e glipre cu xabju

The-typical English-person - dwells-in

… lo fi'ortu'a na.e lo gligugde

… the African-land (Not!)--and the English-country.

The typical English person dwells not in Africa but in England.

The relationship between lo'e cinfo and lo'i cinfo may be explained thus: the typical lion is an imaginary lion-abstraction which best exemplifies the members of the set of lions. There is a similar relationship between le'e and le'i :

[Example 6.27]

le'e xelso merko cu gusta ponse

The-stereotypical Greek-type-of American - is-a-restaurant-type-of owner.

Lots of Greek-Americans own restaurants.

Here we are concerned not with the actual set of Greek-Americans, but with the set of those the speaker has in mind, which is typified by one (real or imaginary) who owns a restaurant. The word "stereotypical" is often derogatory in English, but le'e need not be derogatory in Lojban: it simply suggests that the example is typical in the speaker's imagination rather than in some objectively agreed-upon way. Of course, different speakers may disagree about what the features of "the typical lion" are (some would include having a short intestine, whereas others would know nothing of lions' intestines), so the distinction between lo'e cinfo and le'e cinfo may be very fine.

Furthermore,

[Example 6.28]

le'e skina cu se--finti ne'i la .xali,uyd.

The-stereotypical movie - is-invented in that-named Hollywood.

is probably true to an American, but might be false (not the stereotype) to someone living in India or Russia.

Note that there is no naming equivalent of lo'e and le'e , because there is no need, as a rule, for a "typical George" or a "typical Smith". People or things who share a common name do not, in general, have any other common attributes worth mentioning.


## 6.6 Quantified sumti

The following cmavo are discussed in this section:

ro | PA | all of/each of

su'o | PA | at least (one of)

Quantifiers tell us how many: in the case of quantifiers with sumti, how many things we are talking about. In Lojban, quantifiers are expressed by numbers and mathematical expressions: a large topic discussed in some detail in [chapter-mekso]. For the purposes of this chapter, a simplified treatment will suffice. Our examples will employ either the simple Lojban numbers pa , re , ci , vo , and mu , meaning "one", "two", "three", "four", "five" respectively, or else the two special quantifiers listed above, ro and su'o.

Any sumti may be preceded by an explicit quantifier, which says how many of the things referred to by the sumti the bridi is talking about. Here are some simple examples contrasting sumti with and without explicit quantifiers:

[Example 6.29]

do cadzu lo bisli

You walk-on the ice.

[Example 6.30]

re do cadzu lo bisli

Two-of you walk-on the ice.

The difference between [example-random-id-qL61] and [example-random-id-qLAH] is the presence of the explicit quantifier re in the latter example. Although re by itself means "two" , when used as a quantifier it means "two-of". Out of the listeners (however many there are), two (we are not told which ones) are asserted to be "walkers on the ice". Implicitly, the others (if any) are not walkers on the ice. In Lojban, you cannot say "I own three shoes" if in fact you own four shoes. Numbers need never be specified, but if they are specified they must be correct.

(This rule does not mean that there is no way to specify a number which is vague. The sentence

[Example 6.31]

mi ponse su'o ci cutci

I possess at-least three shoes.

is true if you own three shoes, or four, or indeed any larger number. More details on vague numbers appear in the discussion of mathematical expressions in [chapter-mekso].)

A quantified sumti talks about its things one at a time. When I say [example-random-id-qLAH] , I claim of each of the two that he or she walks on the ice: quantification is inherently distributive, in the sense of [section-masses]. If what I want to say is something two people do jointly, a quantifier is the wrong tool, and the group descriptors of [section-masses] are the right one. This point matters more than it may seem: it is the reason quantifiers and groups are different mechanisms rather than variations on one theme.

What, then, of the unquantified [example-random-id-qL61]? It simply refers to the listeners – however many there are – and says they walk on the ice, without spelling out "each separately" the way ro would:

[Example 6.32]

ro do cadzu lo bisli

All-of you walk-on the ice.

Each of you walks on the ice.

In most contexts [example-random-id-qL61] and [example-random-id-0qr0] come to the same thing, since walking is something people mostly do one by one. But they are not synonyms: [example-random-id-0qr0] insists on the one-at-a-time reading, while [example-random-id-qL61] leaves the manner of walking – single file, arm in arm, in a heap – entirely open.

Here we reach one of the most important rules in this book: a sumti without an explicit quantifier has no quantifier at all. It is a referring expression, pure and simple – in the language of logic, a "constant": a term that names its referents outright, as opposed to a quantified term like re do , which selects from them. The distinction does real work in [chapter-quantifiers] , where quantified terms interact with negation and with each other in ways constants never do. Commit the rule to memory now: no quantifier is present unless you say one.

This rule is part of the ratified article reform; older texts follow a different convention here – see [appendix-changes].


## 6.7 Quantified descriptions

The following cmavo are discussed in this section:

piro | PA | the whole of

pisu'o | PA | a part of

Like other sumti, descriptions can be quantified. When a quantifier appears before a description, it has the same meaning as one appearing before a non-description sumti: it specifies how many things, of all those referred to by the description, are being talked about in this particular bridi. Suppose that context tells us that le gerku refers to three dogs. Then we can say that exactly two of them are white as follows:

[Example 6.33]

re le gerku cu blabi

Two-of the dogs - are-white.

Two of the dogs are white.

When discussing descriptions, this ordinary quantifier is called an "outer quantifier" , since it appears outside the description. But there is another possible location for a quantifier: between the descriptor and the selbri. This quantifier is called an "inner quantifier" , and its meaning is quite different: it tells the listener how many things the description refers to altogether.

For example, the context of [example-random-id-WtUh] supposedly told us that le gerku referred to some three specific dogs. This assumption can be made certain with the use of an explicit inner quantifier:

[Example 6.34]

re le ci gerku cu blabi

Two-of the three dogs - are-white.

Two of the three dogs are white.

(As explained in the discussion of [example-random-id-gLpy] , simple numbers like those in [example-random-id-X3iY] must be exact: it therefore follows that the third dog cannot be white.)

You may also specify an explicit inner quantifier and leave the outer position empty:

[Example 6.35]

le ci gerku cu blabi

The three dogs - are-white.

The three dogs are white.

With no outer quantifier, [example-random-id-f643] simply says of the three dogs that they are white – all of them, as it happens, since whiteness is the sort of thing that holds of each if it holds of them at all.

All of this works identically with lo. The inner quantifier states how many things the description refers to; the outer quantifier picks out how many of them the bridi concerns:

[Example 6.36]

lo mu bakni cu se jirna

The five cows - are-horned.

The five cows have horns.

[Example 6.37]

mu lo pano bakni cu blabi

Five-of the ten cows - are-white.

Five of the ten cows are white.

In [example-random-id-x6Da] we are talking about five cows – which five is left to context, exactly as with an unquantified lo bakni ; the inner mu merely reports their number. In [example-random-id-x6Db] the description refers to ten cows, and the bridi selects five of them.

To restate the essentials: an unquantified description merely refers (see [section-basic-descriptors]), and an inner quantifier on lo counts the things referred to. So lo ci gerku is unremarkable, everyday Lojban for "the three dogs (that we're concerned with)" – it says nothing about how many dogs exist. And because lo carries no quantifier of its own, descriptions get along with negation and quantifier order in the straightforward way described in [chapter-quantifiers].

Pre-reform Lojban reads these quite differently; if you need to read older text closely, see [appendix-changes].

Note how non-veridicality interacts with an inner quantifier on le: le ci nanmu refers to exactly three things – the count is genuine – which the speaker describes as men, rightly or wrongly. The looseness of a le description lies entirely in the selbri; the number of things referred to is stated as fact.

With la , there is no inner quantifier at all: a number string appearing after la is part of the name. Thus la ci bakni is not three of anything; it is somebody or something named "ci bakni" ("Three Cows" – a perfectly good name for a restaurant). An outer quantifier works normally: ci la .magdonaldz. is three of the referents of la .magdonaldz. , say three McDonald's restaurants near my office.

Quantifiers combine with the group descriptors in a way that follows naturally from what inner and outer quantifiers mean. The inner quantifier gives the size of the group; the outer quantifier counts groups:

[Example 6.38]

re loi mu bakni cu bevri lo pipno

Two groups-of five cows - carry a piano.

Two teams of five cows each carried a piano.

[example-random-id-x6Dc] describes two five-cow teams, each team carrying a piano collectively – a compact expression for a rather elaborate scene, and a good illustration of how much the two quantifier positions can do. (It does not, strictly, promise that the two teams share no cows; decent style avoids exploiting that loophole without warning.)

Finally, the fractional quantifiers. Why "pi-"? pi is the Lojban decimal point. Just as pimu means ".5" , a fractional outer quantifier specifies a portion. Applied to a group, it selects a subgroup of that fraction of the size: pimu loi xa bakni is half of a group of six cows – three cows, acting as part of the team. Applied to a set, it selects a subset: pimu le'i nanmu is a subset containing half of the men I have in mind, and is a good way to say "half of the men". (Note that the result is still a set; to refer to the individuals in it, use lu'a , explained in [section-sumti-qualifiers].) Applied to an individual, a fraction gives a part of one thing: pimu le nanmu is half a man, not half of the men – a distinction worth double-checking before speaking. The forms piro and pisu'o accordingly mean "the whole of" and "some part of" , and see their main use with groups and substance-like references: piro loi jdini , all the money; pisu'o loi djacu, some of the water.


## 6.8 Indefinite sumti

By a quirk of Lojban syntax, a quantifier can be followed directly by a selbri, with no descriptor at all; the result is called an "indefinite sumti". (The syntactic mold is exact: an explicit quantifier is required, an inner quantifier is impossible – thus gerku cu blabi is ungrammatical, and so is reboi ci gerku cu blabi , though re ci gerku cu blabi is fine but means "23 dogs are white". Even though no descriptor is present, the elidable terminator ku may still be used.)

[Example 6.39]

ci gerku [ku] cu blabi

Three dogs - are-white.

Three dogs are white.

What does [example-random-id-EsVd] mean? It is a pure statement of quantity: three things which are dogs are white – three dogs anywhere in the world under discussion, not three of some particular dogs already on the conversational table. In the notation of [chapter-quantifiers] , it is exactly equivalent to ci da poi gerku cu blabi : "for exactly three X which are dogs, X is white".

This makes an indefinite sumti subtly different from the corresponding quantified lo description:

[Example 6.40]

ci lo gerku cu blabi

Three-of the dogs - are-white.

Three of the dogs are white.

[example-random-id-gr7Y] quantifies over the referents of lo gerku – the dogs we are talking about, whichever those are in context. If the neighborhood's dogs are under discussion, [example-random-id-gr7Y] says three of them are white, while [example-random-id-EsVd] says only that the world contains exactly three white dogs in all – a much stronger and usually stranger claim, unless context has already narrowed "the world" down to something small. When the referents of lo gerku are understood maximally broadly, the two coincide; in everyday talk they generally do not.

Older texts follow a different convention here; see [appendix-changes].

Note: [example-random-id-gLpy] also contains an indefinite sumti, namely su'o ci cutci: "I own at least three things which are shoes" – a case where quantifying over the whole world is exactly what is wanted, and where the same thing said with su'o ci lo cutci would presuppose some contextually given shoes to count among.


## 6.9 sumti-based descriptions

As stated in [section-basic-descriptors] , most descriptions consist of just a descriptor and a selbri. (In this chapter, the selbri have always been single gismu, but of course any selbri, however complex, can be employed in a description. The syntax and semantics of selbri are explained in [chapter-selbri].) In the intervening sections, inner and outer quantifiers have been added to the syntax. Now it is time to discuss a description of a radically different kind: the sumti-based description.

A sumti-based description has a sumti where the selbri would normally be, and the inner quantifier is required – it cannot be implicit. An outer quantifier is permitted but not required.

A sumti-based description built with le refers to things the speaker describes as being among the referents of the inner sumti; formally, le re do amounts to le re me do, using the me conversion of [chapter-selbri]. Compare the following:

[Example 6.41]

re do cu nanmu

Two-of you - are-men.

[Example 6.42]

le re do cu nanmu

The two-of you - are-men.

[example-random-id-qLaQ] simply specifies that of the listeners, however many they are, two are men. [example-random-id-qLAr] , which has the sumti-based description le re do , refers to two people, described as being among the listeners, and says of them that they are men. The inner quantifier re counts the referents of the whole description – it does not say how many listeners there are altogether.

Here is another group of examples:

[Example 6.43]

re le ci cribe cu bunre

Two-of the three bears - are-brown.

[Example 6.44]

le re le ci cribe cu bunre

The two-of the three bears - are-brown.

[Example 6.45]

pa le re le ci cribe cu bunre

One-of the two-of the three bears - is-brown.

In each case, le ci cribe restricts the bears (or alleged bears) being talked of to three that the speaker has in mind. [example-random-id-qLbf] says that two of them (which two is not stated) are brown. [example-random-id-qLbh] says that a specific pair of them are brown. [example-random-id-qLBq] says that of a specific pair chosen from the original three, one or the other of that pair is brown.


## 6.10 sumti qualifiers

The following cmavo are discussed in this section:

la'e | LAhE | something referred to by

lu'e | LAhE | a reference to

tu'a | LAhE | an abstraction involving

lu'a | LAhE | an individual/member/component of

lu'i | LAhE | a set formed from

lu'o | LAhE | a group formed from

vu'i | LAhE | a sequence formed from

na'ebo | NAhE+BO | something other than

to'ebo | NAhE+BO | the opposite of

no'ebo | NAhE+BO | the neutral form of

je'abo | NAhE+BO | that which indeed is

lu'u | LUhU | elidable terminator for LAhE and NAhE+BO

Well, that's quite a list of cmavo. What are they all about?

The above cmavo and compound cmavo are called the "sumti qualifiers". All of them are either single cmavo of selma'o LAhE, or else compound cmavo involving a scalar negation cmavo of selma'o NAhE immediately followed by bo of selma'o BO. Syntactically, you can prefix a sumti qualifier to any sumti and produce another simple sumti. (You may need to add the elidable terminator lu'u to show where the qualified sumti ends.)

Semantically, sumti qualifiers represent short forms of certain common special cases. Suppose you want to say "I see 'The Red Pony'" , where "The Red Pony" is the title of a book. How about:

[Example 6.46]

mi viska lu lo xunre cmaxirma li'u

I see [quote] the red small-horse [unquote].

But [example-random-id-6W3v] doesn't work: it says that you see a piece of text "The Red Pony". That might be all right if you were looking at the cover of the book, where the words "The Red Pony" are presumably written. (More precisely, where the words lo xunre cmaxirma are written – but we may suppose the book has been translated into Lojban.)

What you really want to say is:

[Example 6.47]

mi viska lo selsinxa

I see the thing-represented-by

… be lu lo xunre cmaxirma li'u

… - [quote] the red small-horse [unquote].

The x2 place of selsinxa (the x1 place of sinxa) is a sign or symbol, and the x1 place of selsinxa (the x2 place of sinxa) is the thing represented by the sign. [example-random-id-MSVK] allows us to use a symbol (namely the title of a book) to represent the thing it is a symbol of (namely the book itself).

This operation turns out to be needed often enough that it's useful to be able to say:

[Example 6.48]

mi viska la'e lu lo xunre cmaxirma li'u [lu'u]

I see the-referent-of [quote] the red small-horse [unquote] -.

So when la'e is prefixed to a sumti referring to a symbol, it produces a sumti referring to the referent of that symbol. (In computer jargon, la'e dereferences a pointer.)

By introducing a sumti qualifier, we correct a false sentence ( [example-random-id-6W3v]), which too closely resembles its literal English equivalent, into a true sentence ( [example-random-id-Ajty]), without having to change it overmuch; in particular, the structure remains the same. Most of the uses of sumti qualifiers are of this general kind.

The sumti qualifier lu'e provides the converse operation: it can be prefixed to a sumti referring to some thing to produce a sumti referring to a sign or symbol for the thing. For example,

[Example 6.49]

mi pu cusku lu'e lo vi cukta

I [past] express a-symbol-for the nearby book.

I said the title of this book.

The equivalent form not using a sumti qualifier would be:

[Example 6.50]

mi pu cusku lo sinxa be lo vi cukta

I [past] express the symbol-for - the nearby book.

which is equivalent to [example-random-id-7ytm] , but longer.

The other sumti qualifiers follow the same rules. The cmavo tu'a is used in forming abstractions, and is explained more fully in [section-sumti-raising]. The triplet lu'a , lu'i , and lu'o convert between individuals, sets, and groups; vu'i belongs to this group as well, but creates a sequence, which is similar to a set but has a definite order. (The set of John and Charles is the same as the set of Charles and John, but the sequences are different.) Here are some examples:

[Example 6.51]

mi troci tu'a lo vorme

I try some-abstraction-about the door.

I try (to open) the door.

[example-random-id-ioCu] might mean that I try to do something else involving the door; the form is deliberately vague.

Most of the following examples make use of the cmavo ri , belonging to selma'o KOhA. This cmavo means "the thing last mentioned" ; it is equivalent to repeating the immediately previous sumti (but in its original context). It is explained in more detail in [section-ri-gohi-series].

[Example 6.52]

lo'i ratcu cu barda

The-set-of rats - is-large.

.iku'i lu'a ri cmalu

But members-of it-last-mentioned are-small.

The set of rats is large, but its members are small (individually).

[Example 6.53]

lo ratcu cu cmalu .iku'i lu'i ri barda

Rats - are-small. But the-set-formed-from them-last-mentioned is-large.

Rats are small, but the set of them is large.

[Example 6.54]

mi ce do girzu

I in-a-set-with you are-a-set.

… .i lu'o ri gunma

… - The-group-of it-last-mentioned is-a-group.

… .i vu'i ri porsi

… - The-sequence-of it-last-mentioned is-a-sequence

The set of you and me is a set. The group of you and me is a group. The sequence of you and me is a sequence.

(Yes, I know these examples are a bit silly. This set was introduced for completeness, and practical examples are as yet hard to come by.)

Finally, the four sumti qualifiers formed from a cmavo of NAhE and bo are all concerned with negation, which is discussed in detail in [chapter-negation]. Here are a few examples of negation sumti qualifiers:

mi viska na'ebo lo gerku I see something-other-than the dog.

This compound, na'ebo , is the most common of the four negation sumti qualifiers. The others usually only make sense in the context of repeating, with modifications, something already referred to:

[Example 6.55]

mi nelci lo glare cidja

I like hot-type-of food.

… .ije do nelci to'ebo ri

… And you like the-opposite-of the-last-mentioned.

… .ije la .djein. nelci no'ebo ra

… And that-named Jane likes the-neutral-value-of something-mentioned.

I like hot food, and you like cold food, and Jane likes lukewarm food.

(In [example-random-id-JwCb] , the sumti ra refers to some previously mentioned sumti other than that referred to by ri. We cannot use ri here, because it would signify la .djein. , that being the most recent sumti available to ri. See more detailed explanations in [section-ri-gohi-series].)


## 6.11 The syntax of vocative phrases

Vocative phrases are not sumti, but are explained in this chapter because their syntax is very similar to that of sumti. Grammatically, a vocative phrase is one of the so-called "free modifiers" of Lojban, along with subscripts, parentheses, and various other constructs explained in [chapter-structure]. They can be placed after many, but not all, constructions of the grammar: in general, after any elidable terminator (which, however, must not then be elided!), at the beginnings and ends of sentences, and in many other places.

The purpose of a vocative phrase is to indicate who is being addressed, or to indicate to that person that he or she ought to be listening. A vocative phrase begins with a cmavo of selma'o COI or DOI, all of which are explained in more detail in [section-vocative-scales]. Sometimes that is all there is to the phrase:

[Example 6.56]

coi

[greetings]

Hello.

[Example 6.57]

je'e

[acknowledgement]

Uh-huh.

Roger!

In these cases, the person being addressed is obvious from the context. However, a vocative word (more precisely, one or more cmavo of COI, possibly followed by doi , or else just doi by itself) can be followed by one of several kinds of phrases, all of which are intended to indicate the addressee. The most common case is a cmevla (name-word):

[Example 6.58]

coi .djan.

[greetings] John.

Hello, John.

Using doi instead is like just saying someone's name to attract his or her attention:

[Example 6.59]

doi .djan.

O John.

John!

In place of a cmevla, a description may appear, lacking its descriptor, which is understood to be le :

[Example 6.60]

coi xunre pastu nixli

Hello, (red-type-of dress)-type-of girl.

Hello, girl with the red dress!

The listener need not really be a xunre pastu nixli , as long as she understands herself correctly from the description. (Actually, only a bare selbri can appear; explicit quantifiers are forbidden in this form of vocative.)

Finally, a complete sumti may be used, the most general case.

[Example 6.61]

co'o la .bab. .e la .noras.

[partings] that-named Bob and that-named Nora.

Goodbye, Bob and Nora.

[example-random-id-V530] is thus the same as:

[Example 6.62]

coi le xunre pastu nixli

Hello, the-one-described-as (red-type-of dress)-type-of girl!

and [example-random-id-ULHn] is the same as:

[Example 6.63]

doi la .djan.

O that-named John!

Finally, the elidable terminator for vocative phrases is do'u (of selma'o DOhU), which is rarely needed except when a simple vocative word is being placed somewhere within a bridi. It may also be required when a vocative is placed between a sumti and its relative clause, or when there are a sequence of so-called "free modifiers" (vocatives, subscripts, utterance ordinals – see [chapter-mekso] – metalinguistic comments – see [section-parentheses] – or reciprocals – see [chapter-structure]) which must be properly separated.

The meaning of a vocative phrase that is within a sentence is not affected by its position in the sentence: thus [example-random-id-qLFi] and [example-random-id-qLGC] mean the same thing:

[Example 6.64]

doi .djan. ko klama mi

O John you--[imperative] go-to me.

John, come to me!

[Example 6.65]

ko klama mi doi .djan.

You--[imperative] go-to me O John.

Come to me, John!

As usual for this chapter, the full syntax of vocative phrases has not been explained: relative clauses, discussed in [chapter-relative-clauses] , make for more possibilities.


## 6.12 Lojban names

Names have been used freely as sumti throughout this chapter without too much explanation. The time for the explanation has now come.

First of all, there are two different kinds of things usually called "names" when talking about Lojban. The naming predicates of [section-basic-descriptors] are just ordinary predicates which are being used in a special sense. In addition, though, there is a class of Lojban words which are used only to name things: these can be recognized by the fact that they end in a consonant and are surrounded by pauses. Some examples:

[Example 6.66]

.djan. .meris. .djein. .alis.

John. Mary. Jane. Alice.

Names of this kind have two basic uses in Lojban: when used in a vocative phrase (see [section-vocative-syntax]) they indicate who the listener is or should be. When used with a descriptor of selma'o LA, namely la , lai , or la'i , they form sumti which refer to the persons or things known by the name.

[Example 6.67]

la .djonz. klama lo zarci

Those-named Jones go-to the store.

The Joneses go to-the store.

[Example 6.68]

lai .djonz. klama lo zarci

The-group-of-those-named Jones goes-to the store.

The Joneses go to the store.

In [example-random-id-qLgw] , the significance is that the persons (perhaps only one) I mean to refer to by the name .djonz. are going to the store – each of them, or together, as context suggests. In [example-random-id-qLHn] , the Joneses go as a group: the family outing, not necessarily every family member (see [section-masses]). Of course, by .djonz. I can mean whomever I want: that person need not use the name .djonz. at all.

The sumti in [example-random-id-qLgw] and [example-random-id-qLHn] operate exactly like the similar uses of la and lai in [example-random-id-PrGp] and [example-random-id-H8z5] respectively. The only difference is that these descriptors are followed by Lojban name-words (i.e. cmevla). And in fact, the only difference between descriptors of selma'o LA (these three) and of selma'o LE (all the other descriptors) is that the former can be followed by name-words, whereas the latter cannot.

Unless some other rule prevents it (such as the rule that zo is always followed by a single word, which is quoted), multiple name-words may appear wherever one name-word is permitted, each with its terminating pause:

[Example 6.69]

doi .djan.--.pol.--.djonz. lo bloti cu klama fi--la .ni'uport.--.ni'uz.

O John--Paul--Jones the boat - goes from-that-named Newport--News.

John Paul Jones, the boat comes (to somewhere) from Newport News.

A name-word may not contain any consonant combination that is illegal in Lojban words generally: the "impermissible consonant clusters" of Lojban morphology (explained in [section-clusters]). Thus .djeimz. is not a valid version of "James" (because mz is invalid): .djeimyz. will suffice.

Names may be borrowed from other languages or created arbitrarily. Another common practice is to use one or more rafsi, arranged to end with a consonant, to form a name: thus the rafsi loj- for logji (logical) and ban- for bangu (language) unite to form the name of this language:

[Example 6.70]

.lojban.

Lojban

When borrowing names from another language which end in a vowel, or when turning a Lojban brivla (all of which end in vowels) into a cmevla, the vowel may be removed or an arbitrary consonant added. It is common (but not required) to use the consonants s or n when borrowing vowel-final names from English; speakers of other languages may wish to use other consonant endings.


## 6.13 Pro-sumti summary

The Lojban pro-sumti are the cmavo of selma'o KOhA. They fall into several classes: personal, definable, quantificational, reflexive, back-counting, indefinite, demonstrative, metalinguistic, relative, question. More details are given in [chapter-anaphoric-cmavo] ; this section mostly duplicates information found there, summarizing each class from the sumti point of view.

The following examples illustrate each of the classes. Pro-sumti are referring expressions, exactly like descriptions: they carry no hidden quantifiers (see [section-quantified-sumti]). An explicit quantifier selects from a pro-sumti's referents in the usual way – re do is two of the listeners – so it is possible to restrict, but naturally not to extend, what a pro-sumti refers to.

Personal pro-sumti ( mi , do , mi'o , mi'a , ma'a , do'o , ko) refer to the speaker or the listener or both, with or without third parties:

[Example 6.71]

mi prami do

I love you.

The plural personal pro-sumti mi'o , mi'a , ma'a , and do'o refer to the relevant combinations of people (you and I, I and others, you and I and others, you and others). As with any plural reference, whether they satisfy a selbri one by one or together is left to context (see [section-masses]); mi'o bevri le pipno is most naturally the two of us carrying it together.

Definable pro-sumti ( ko'a , ko'e , ko'i , ko'o , ko'u , fo'a , fo'e , fo'i , fo'o , fo'u) refer to whatever the speaker has explicitly made them refer to. This reference is accomplished with goi (of selma'o GOI), which means "defined-as".

[Example 6.72]

lo cribe goi ko'a cu xekri .i ko'a citka lo smacu

The bear defined-as it-1 - is-black. - It-1 eats the mouse.

Quantificational pro-sumti ( da , de , di) are used as variables in bridi involving predicate logic:

[Example 6.73]

ro da poi prenu

All somethings-1 which are-persons

… cu prami pa de poi finpe

… - love one something-2 which is-a-fish.

All persons love a fish (each his/her own).

(This is not the same as "All persons love a certain fish" ; the difference between the two is one of quantifier order.) The quantification conventions for the da-series are particular to it (roughly: a bare da means su'o da on first use, and keeps referring to the same things thereafter), and are discussed in detail in [chapter-quantifiers].

Reflexive pro-sumti ( vo'a , vo'e , vo'i , vo'o , vo'u) refer to the same referents as sumti filling other places in the same bridi, with the effect that the same thing is referred to twice:

[Example 6.74]

lo cribe cu batci vo'a

The bear - bites what-is-in-the-x1-place.

The bear bites itself.

Back-counting pro-sumti ( ri , ra , ru) refer to the referents of previous sumti counted backwards from the pro-sumti:

[Example 6.75]

mi klama la .frankfurt. ri

I go-to that-named Frankfurt from-the-referent-of-the-last-sumti

I go from Frankfurt to Frankfurt (by some unstated route).

Indefinite pro-sumti ( zo'e , zu'i , zi'o) refer to something which is unspecified:

[Example 6.76]

mi klama la .frankfurt.

I go-to that-named Frankfurt

… zo'e zo'e zo'e

… from-unspecified via-unspecified by-means-unspecified.

zo'e simply refers to whatever unspecified thing or things make the bridi sensible in context – the value the speaker couldn't be bothered to spell out. It is a referring expression like any other, and indeed every description can be thought of as a zo'e with an explanation attached: lo zarci is, in effect, "zo'e which is a market".

Demonstrative pro-sumti ( ti , ta , tu) refer to things pointed at by the speaker, or when pointing is not possible, to things near or far from the speaker:

[Example 6.77]

ko muvgau

You--[imperative] move

… ti ta tu

… this-thing from-that-nearby-place to-that-further-away-place.

Move this from there to over there!

Metalinguistic pro-sumti ( di'u , de'u , da'u , di'e , de'e , da'e , dei , do'i) refer to spoken or written utterances, either preceding, following, or the same as the current utterance.

[Example 6.78]

li re su'i re du li vo

The-number two plus two equals the-number four.

… .i la'e di'u jetnu

… - The-referent-of the-previous-utterance is-true.

The relative pro-sumti ( ke'a) is used within relative clauses (see [chapter-relative-clauses] for a discussion of relative clauses) to refer to whatever sumti the relative clause is attached to.

[Example 6.79]

mi viska lo mlatu ku poi zo'e

I see the cat(s) - such-that something-unspecified

… zbasu ke'a lo slasi

… makes it/them-(the-cats) from plastic.

I see the cat(s) made of plastic.

The question pro-sumti ( ma) is used to ask questions which request the listener to supply a sumti which will make the question into a truth:

[Example 6.80]

do klama ma

You go-to what-sumti?

Where are you going?

In addition, sequences of lerfu words (of selma'o BY and related selma'o) can also be used as definable pro-sumti.


## 6.14 Quotation summary

There are four kinds of quotation in Lojban: text quotation, words quotation, single-word quotation, non-Lojban quotation. More information is provided in [chapter-structure].

Text quotations are preceded by lu and followed by li'u , and are an essential part of the surrounding text: they must be grammatical Lojban texts.

[Example 6.81]

mi cusku lu mi'e .djan. li'u

I say the-text--[quote] I-am John [unquote].

I say "I'm John".

Words quotations are quotations of one or more Lojban words. The words need not mean anything, but they must be morphologically valid so that the end of the quotation can be discerned.

[Example 6.82]

mi cusku lo'u li--mi le'u

I say the-words--[quote] li mi [unquote].

I say "li mi".

Note that the translation of [example-random-id-UMDQ] does not translate the Lojban words, because they are not presumed to have any meaning (in fact, they are ungrammatical).

Single-word quotation quotes a single Lojban word. Compound cmavo are not allowed.

[Example 6.83]

mi cusku zo .ai

I say the-word .ai.

Non-Lojban quotation can quote anything, Lojban or not, even non-speech such as drum talk, whistle words, music, or belching. A Lojban word which does not appear within the quotation is used before and after it to set it off from the surrounding Lojban text.

[Example 6.84]

mi cusku zoi .ku'ot. I'm--John .ku'ot.

I express [non-Lojban] < I'm--John >.

I say "I'm John".

Like all other sumti, quotations are referring expressions with no hidden quantifiers: a quotation refers to the quoted words themselves.


## 6.15 Number summary

The sumti which refer to numbers consist of the cmavo li (of selma'o LI) followed by an arbitrary Lojban mekso, or mathematical expression. This can be anything from a simple number up to the most complicated combination of numbers, variables, operators, and so on. Much more information on numbers is given in [chapter-mekso]. Here are a few examples of increasing complexity:

[Example 6.85]

li vo

the-number four

4

[Example 6.86]

li re su'i re

the-number two plus two

2 + 2

[Example 6.87]

li .abu bi'epi'i xy. bi'ete'a re su'i by. bi'epi'i xy. su'i cy.

the-number a times x to-power 2 plus b times x plus c

ax 2 + bx + c

An alternative to li is me'o , also of selma'o LI. Number expressions beginning with me'o refer to the actual expression, rather than its value. Thus [example-random-id-qLIm] and [example-random-id-qLis] above have the same meaning, the number four, whereas

[Example 6.88]

me'o vo

the-expression four

"4"

and

[Example 6.89]

me'o re su'i re

the-expression two plus two

"2+2"

refer to different pieces of text.

Like all other sumti, these are referring expressions with no hidden quantifiers: a li sumti refers to a number, a me'o sumti to a piece of text. The difference matters when counting: there is only one number which is 4, but there are many texts "4" , as many as there are documents in which that numeral appears.
