## 22.1 What this chapter is, and is not

The main reference chapters of this book teach this edition's selected account of Lojban, preserving each rule's status where it is taught – ratified, committee-approved, checkpointed, de facto, or unsettled. This chapter describes something else: words and grammatical variants that no body ever adopted, but that a reader of real Lojban text will sooner or later meet. We call a single unadopted word an "experimental cmavo" , and a coherent set of unadopted grammar changes a "dialect". Nothing in this chapter is official, and nothing in it is needed to speak or read standard Lojban; but a reference that pretended these things did not exist would be describing the language as it might have been, not as it is used.

Standard Lojban itself provides the space these words live in. [chapter-morphology] explains that cmavo word-shapes beyond the simple assigned forms – longer vowel strings such as zo'oi or su'oi – are reserved for experimental use: they are grammatical word-forms with no officially assigned meaning. Coining one breaks no rule; the coinage simply has whatever standing its users give it. The community's dictionaries record such words, clearly marked as unofficial, and since deliberate change to the official language has ceased (see [chapter-about]), this decentralized process is where all new function words now come from.

The boundary between experimental and adopted has moved before. The rule, used throughout this book, that every cmevla is written with a leading and trailing dot began life as a deliberately partisan dialect – its very name, "the dot side" , was a joke – and is now committee-approved (the details are in [appendix-changes]). The established corpus is itself an accretion of once-experimental proposals adopted at various levels; the difference now is that the bodies which could promote them no longer meet. The features in this chapter are, in that sense, proposals with no one left to propose them to: their standing rises and falls with usage alone.

What earns a feature a place in this chapter? Two things, both stated in each section: usage evidence – the feature appears in genuine text or established community writing, not merely in a dictionary entry – and machine support – at least one maintained parser accepts it. The parsers matter because they are how the community actually delimits its dialects. The standard camxes grammar accepts only standard Lojban; its "beta" variant adds the popular, backward-compatible experimental cmavo; further variants layer on the genuine grammar changes described at the end of this chapter. Other tools take the opposite approach and accept the popular experimental cmavo by default, reserving explicit dialect switches for the true grammar changes. Every example in this chapter has been verified against at least one such parser, and each section says which.

All of them are unofficial – neither ratified, committee-approved, nor checkpointed. Where a section cites usage frequency, the underlying evidence is a 2015 six-corpus community survey whose counts overweight chat logs and individual authors; treat it as rough rank order, not endorsement.


## 22.2 Quoting the outside world: zo'oi and friends

The following experimental cmavo are discussed in this section. (In this chapter's cmavo lists, a starred selma'o name marks an unofficial assignment: either an experimental selma'o, like ZOhOI*, or an experimental addition to a standard one, like PA* later on.)

zo'oi | ZOhOI* | quote next word (non-Lojban)

la'oi | ZOhOI* | name from next word (non-Lojban)

ra'oi | ZOhOI* | quote next word as a rafsi

me'oi | MEhOI* | ad-hoc brivla from next word

Standard Lojban quotes non-Lojban material with zoi ... , which requires choosing a delimiting word and saying it twice ([section-more-quotations]). For a single foreign word, that ceremony is heavy, and some of the best-attested experimental cmavo shortcut it: zo'oi quotes exactly one following word, delimited by pauses in speech or whitespace in writing, just as zo does for a single Lojban word:

[Example 22.1]

zo'oi small cu glico valsi

[quote-one] "small" - is-an-English word.

"Small" is an English word.

Its sibling la'oi does the same for names, standing to la'o as zo'oi stands to zoi. In the community's 2015 frequency survey it ranked first among all experimental cmavo – unsurprisingly, since foreign one-word names (of people, software, websites) are everywhere:

[Example 22.2]

mi nelci la'oi Bach

I like that-named "Bach".

I like Bach.

Two rarer members complete the family. me'oi makes an ad-hoc predicate out of a foreign word – the community calls the result a "stage-0 fu'ivla" , a borrowing that skips even the minimal Lojbanization of [section-fuhivla]:

[Example 22.3]

lo xirma ca me'oi gallop

The horse [present] is-a-"gallop"-er.

The horse is galloping.

and ra'oi quotes a single word as a rafsi, useful because many rafsi are not words at all and so cannot be quoted with zo:

[Example 22.4]

ra'oi bra cu rafsi zo barda

[quote-rafsi] "bra" - is-a-rafsi-of the-word "barda".

The rafsi "bra" belongs to "barda".

Status and support: all four are attested in genuine text – la'oi and zo'oi heavily, me'oi and ra'oi more thinly. The standard camxes grammar rejects all four; the camxes beta family and jbotci accept them, jbotci without any dialect switch.


## 22.3 Plural quantifiers: su'oi and ro'oi

The following experimental cmavo are discussed in this section:

su'oi | PA* | existential plural quantifier

ro'oi | PA* | universal plural quantifier

The official quantifiers su'o and ro are singular: a variable they bind stands for one individual at a time ([chapter-quantifiers]). That leaves a genuine gap. Some predicates are true of several things taken together without being true of any one of them – gathering, surrounding, being-a-twosome – and a claim like "some people are gathering" cannot be built from a singular quantifier at all: no single person gathers. Standard Lojban fills the gap with constants – an unquantified lo prenu can refer to people jointly, and loi forms groups – but it has no way to quantify plurally.

The experimental pair su'oi and ro'oi supplies exactly that: a variable they bind may stand for one or more things at once. With them one can say:

[Example 22.5]

su'oi da poi prenu cu jmaji

Some-things X which are-people - gather.

Some people are gathering.

where the da stands for the gatherers jointly. Each member of the pair is definable from the other by the usual duality: su'oi da is na ku ro'oi da na ku , exactly parallel to the singular rules of [chapter-quantifiers].

The standard camxes grammar rejects these quantifiers; the camxes beta family and jbotci accept them. The rigorous writing that uses them is the work of a small circle of theorists rather than everyday text, and this book's logic chapters are written entirely with the official singular quantifiers.

What was ratified is the number-neutral constant semantics of the modern lo ; construing that semantics as the constant half of a formal plural-logic system is an unofficial analysis, and these quantifiers – the system's other half – remain unadopted proposals (see [appendix-changes]).


## 22.4 Other experimental cmavo in real use

Beyond the quotation family, the community's frequency surveys put one coherent set clearly ahead of the rest: the error-correction quotes lo'ai , sa'ai , and le'ai. Standard Lojban's erasure words ([section-erasure]) can only erase the most recent words; in live conversation one often wants to reach back and patch something said a sentence ago. The set works like a search-and-replace: lo'ai quotes the wrong text, sa'ai quotes its replacement, and le'ai closes the correction:

[Example 22.6]

mi klama lo zarci .i lo'ai zarci sa'ai zdani le'ai

I go-to a market. [wrong:] "market" [replace-with:] "house" [correction-end]

I went to the market – I mean, the house.

Two single words have also earned real currency. xo'e is the elliptical digit: what zo'e is to sumti, xo'e is to numbers – an unspecified quantity the speaker declines to give:

[Example 22.7]

xo'e prenu cu klama

[some-number-of] people - come.

Some number of people are coming.

And zo'ei (of LAhE-like grammar) means "something associated with ..." – roughly zo'e pe – and is often reached for where a lazy speaker might otherwise stretch tu'a beyond its abstraction sense: mi viska zo'ei la .paris. , "I see something Paris-related" – a photograph, perhaps, or the skyline.

A support caveat: no camxes grammar recognizes zo'ei as a word – the string collides with a zo quotation there – and the danger is silent: a full sentence like the one above still parses in camxes, as a quotation of "'ei" with a different structure entirely. jbotci recognizes it properly.

Several more circulate with thinner but real use, and this book records them only by name: the well-wishing vocative di'ai ; the selma'o-quoter ma'oi (ma'oi ba names selma'o PU by example); the imperative marker ko'oi ; the aspectual xa'o ("already"); and the abstractor poi'i , common in the plural-logic literature of [section-dialects-plural].

Status and support: the error-correction set and xo'e are accepted by the camxes beta family and by jbotci; zo'ei by jbotci only; of the named mentions, the camxes beta family accepts them, while jbotci's support varies word by word – verify before relying on any. None is official.


## 22.5 The cmevla–brivla merger

The dialects so far add words. The remaining two change grammar. The cmevla–brivla merger (CBM) abolishes the grammatical wall between name-words and predicate words: under it, a cmevla like .djan. may serve as a selbri or tanru unit – predicating of its subject, with the precise place structure left to the dialect's users – and the article la becomes an ordinary descriptor taking whatever selbri follows. The motivating complaint is pedagogical: in standard Lojban la behaves one way before a cmevla and another way before a brivla, and learners who discover that predicates can name things are tempted to write what the standard grammar forbids. Under CBM this sentence – rejected by standard camxes and by jbotci's default grammar – is fine:

[Example 22.8]

ti .djan.

This is-a-John.

This John-s, so to speak. (CBM dialect only)

Because the merger reinterprets existing forms rather than only adding new ones, it is not backward-compatible: the same string of standard Lojban can parse differently under CBM, and the two tested implementations (the camxes "cbm" grammars; jbotci's "cbm" dialect switch) do not agree in every corner about how aggressively to reinterpret la phrases. What they agree on is the new permission itself: a cmevla may predicate.

Status and support: a real, parser-implemented dialect with an established design literature; more discussed than written – running text composed wholly in CBM is scarce. In the tested tools it requires an explicit dialect grammar or switch.


## 22.6 Short words for common meanings: ce-ki-tau-jau

The last dialect is a family of cmavo swaps, known by the label ce-ki-tau-jau. Its diagnosis: some of the language's busiest cmavo have two syllables ( ce'u , ke'a , tu'a , jo'u , du'u) while some one-syllable forms are nearly idle ( ce , ki , tau , jau , du). The dialect swaps each pair: in ce-ki-tau-jau text, ce means what ce'u means in this book, and vice versa – each swap bidirectional in principle and separately adoptable. (In practice, the community's own record notes, the swaps mostly run one way: the short forms get the busy meanings as synonyms, while the long forms also keep their old senses.)

The trap is that most of the short forms are meaningful in standard Lojban too, so a swapped sentence often parses in both grammars with different meanings: lo broda poi ki broda is a sticky-tense construction to a standard parser and a relative clause with ke'a to a ce-ki-tau one. Text in this dialect is therefore unintelligible to a standard reader in a peculiarly silent way – nothing fails; it just means something else. Well-behaved dialect text announces itself first (the community convention is a header like jo'au tcekitaujau).

Neither tested implementation matches the primary five-pair proposal exactly. The camxes "ckt" grammar implements the ce / ki / tau / du swaps plus several others, but not jau. jbotci's "ce-ki-tau-jau" dialect implements ce / ki / tau / jau , adds a su'o-to- su swap, and omits du – and it helpfully prints the standard equivalents back.

By the community's own assessment the dialect is very little used; it is included here as the clearest worked example of what a dialect is. This book's own text, of course, uses none of the swaps.
