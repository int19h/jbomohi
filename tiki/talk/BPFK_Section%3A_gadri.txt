My disagreement with this pages is almost entirely about {lo], so I will get back to that.
on {lo'e} and {le'e}.These do NOT take external quantifiers; internal quantifiers are about the size of group which is being typed or stereotyped (and so is rarely used).  Further, neither of these supports existential generalization: the fact that the typical whatever does so and so does not mean that any whatever does (or even, I think, that there are whatevers.  If we want to talk about more than one typical whatever, the appropriate form (even if it is only one but is not being used for typing) is to used the (as yet unlexed) brivla for "typuical" and "stereotypical".

Along the lines of these, a modern language surely needs corresponding forms for "the average," probably one for each measure of central tendency>  Of these, the mean does not support generalization but both median and mode do, so this fact should be made visible somehow (maybe, sticking to the rules, {xo'e} for mean and have the others look more like normaler gadri: {xa'a} and {xa'e} say).

I am unsure about the following additions.  Trying to deal with them without much care is what has complicated {lo} out of coherence.  On the other hand, I suspect tha, once we master subjunctives in Lojban, the problem cases will be dealt with. Until then (and maybe even after then as a shortcut -- like {lo'e} and {le'e} (although admittedly we cannot now say what is abbreviated in these case)):
a gadri, {xo'o} say, which produces the name of the species/kind/... of things that satisfy the predicate.  Such things always exist, even if there are no such critters as satisy the predicate.  These can be used to make general claims of one sort.
And another for the stuff/substance/goo of things of the predicated sort.  This is useful for another kind of generalization (though less common, I think, except for the normally mass nouns).  

Once the extension of old {lo} are dealt with separately, old {lo} can take on its proper job again, doing what has rather less successfully been done by most uses of new {lo}: an unspecified member of the current domain that actually has the proberty described.  As such, it is generalizable **within the range of the current domain** but not generally outside.  I would propose a minor modification to the traditional description, namely, that in other domains that the this-world one, {su'o} could be used to indicate that the object involved exists also in the this-world domain (with essentially the same characteristics).  Where this would allow for fronting of burtied quantifiers is unclear, but at elast it opens the possibility, which identifying {lo} and {su'o} in opaque contexts does not.  
I will comment later on whether this shift back to the old meaning keeps all the examples given. 

## 2004-05-29T03:38:08Z — PierreAbbat (@PierreAbbat) (post 609)

{lo pare sovda cu fepni li 42} to me means that there are 12 eggs, each of which costs 42 cents. If a dozen eggs costs 42 cents, I'd say {lo sovda paremei cu fepni li 42} or maybe {loi pare sovda cu fepni li 42}.

{lo'e se} with the word for a kind of organism is a special case and should be mentioned. It refers to the taxon containing all members of the kind and no others, if that exists. So {lo'e se guzme} means the family Cucurbitaceae, while {lo se guzme} can be Cucumis, Sicyos, Luffa, or any of several others. {lo'e se jesymabru}, however, has no clear meaning, as {jesymabru} can refer to both spiny anteaters and hedgehogs.

## 2004-06-26T14:21:53Z — pycyn (@pycyn) (post 1506)

Title: Default quantifiers
Message-Id: pycyn-0-655262cb08@www.lojban.org

The problem with doing away with default quantifiers is that ther is always some implicit quqntifier involved for any reference to a distributive group.  Thus, when negation is applied -- or when other quanifiers are to be passed -- some meaning does change.  I think the idea is to apply the default quantifiers in situ when uttered, not to have them firmly in place to be affected in the way just mentioned.  But that gives some odd results, essentially placing such quantifiers at the very front of an utterance, never mind where they physically occur: so {le broda cu brode} means that each of things things I am calling brodas is a brode and {le broda na brode} means that each of them is not, rather than, say, that not all of them are (which is what it might seem to say).  If that is the intention, then it is a bad idea for the obvious reasons that 1) negation doesn't work as expected and 2)there are common situations which are not covered (cases where some of le broda are brode and some are not.  If we seriously mean that there is no quantifier when none is explicit, then we are at a loss to say what {le broda cu brode} means; it seems to require minimally that {su'o le broda cu brode} is true (we would not say it if NO so-called broda were brode). But then, what does {le broda na brode} mean: that all broda fail to be brode?  Or (back to the previous suggestion) that some are not (maybe all).  This latter is clearly the better sugggestion in that it does not exclude any cases, but it still leaves negation rather odd (but in a way that goes slaong with "there are no quantifiers there").  It tends to reduce {le broda na brode}, contradictory negation, to {le broda naku brode}, contrary negation, which amounts to saying that there must be le brode.  Now, for {le brode} this is a plausible position to take, since you can't select among non-existents (it is said).  But if the same sort of rules are applied to {lo broda} the case is less clear, since {lo broda cu brode} can clearly be false (or at least not true) because there are no broda, in which case the two negations do not collapse together.  (Well, there are ways to make at least the proposition {go lo broda na brode gi lo broda naku brode} true even then, but it does not seem that Lojban want to do them.)  Interestingly, both of these suggestions makee {lo broda} (and {le} too) into particular quantifiers at the beginning of utterance -- maximum context leapers.  This apparently contrasts with the entity reading of {lo broda} which tended to make {lo broda} a particular quantifier with shortest possible scope (each occurrence covered separately except the same-referent anaphores).  The broad-scope version implies the narrow-scope, but not conversely, so -- if we must do this -- is the one to choose.
