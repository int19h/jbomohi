On Mon, Sep 29, 2008 at 1:08 PM, tijlan <picos.picos@gmail.com> wrote:
>
> I suppose that is because {-} isn't counted as a consonant equal to
> {s} in the first place. What if the parser is designed so that it

Each consonant is treated (slightly) different, so "just like s" would
limit it to "s". It could treat it more or less like a new consonant.
One context where this could be a problem is with the word used as
zoi-delimiter. zoi (word) .... (word). Since {saras} and {sarat} are
two different words, using {sara-} as an ambiguous notation that could
stand for either can be a problem. The different allophones of "r" are
not really a comparable situation, because "r" is just one phoneme, no
matter which allophone you use to realize it. But different consonants
at the end of a name result in different names.

I usually consider the etymology when Lojbanizing names. For example, if the
name is 'Hannah' (Hebrew), I would go with {xanat.}, because Lojban {x} is
the sound of the initial consonant of the Hebrew word, & because in other
Semitic languages, feminine nouns end with a {t} sound. While if the name is
'Helena' (Greek), I would go with {.elenas.}, because the Greek begins with
a "rough breathing" (like English 'h') which is no longer pronounced, and an
{s} sound marks the nominative singular in many Indo European languages
(maybe just for masculine nouns, but what are you gonna do?).

"-s" is also the feminine singular genitive ending in Greek (though the alpha 
often turns to eta, at least in Attic-derived dialects) and "-n", the other 
generally added consonant, is the accusative ending.

For Basque names, I add "-k", which is both the ergative ending and part of 
the local genitive ending. E.g. {la pablos.saratcagek. zvati la donostiak.}
