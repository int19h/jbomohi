I disagree. My wife and I jokingly lie to each other all the time.
Her: did you eat the last piece of cake?
Me:  ~looks around shadily~ uhhh... no?

I know she won't be deceived, and I don't actually intend to deceive.  I'm
intentionally telling an untruth in a way that's so bad that I'm effectively
comminicating to her that I have eaten the cake. But the word that I said
WAS untrue.

Furthermore, if I believe that the sky is pink but I say "the sky is blue"
then even though I have said a true expression, I would still call that a
lie.

These are why I understand a lie to be a statement that describes reality as
being different from the reality that the speaker believes to be true.
Intent to deceive isn't important and actual truthiness of the statement
doesn't matter either.

Luke Bergen, On 02/07/2011 17:59:
> I disagree.

Before disagreeing, or in order to disagree more intelligently, read Coleman & Kay 1981 "Prototype semantics and the English word _lie_", <http://www.jstor.org/pss/414285>.

In a nutshell: in prototype models of semantics, category membership (such as being a lie) is a matter of degree. The extent to which something involves intent to deceive affects the extent to which it counts as a lie.
